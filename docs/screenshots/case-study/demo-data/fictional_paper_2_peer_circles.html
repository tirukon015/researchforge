Peer-Explanation Circles and Statistics Anxiety: A Mixed-Methods Study

Tomas Reyes-Lind, Priya Varma and Elin Sundqvist
Centre for Quantitative Education, Marrowgate College (fictional)

Abstract

Statistics anxiety is associated with avoidance and lower attainment in introductory courses. We evaluated peer-explanation circles, weekly 45-minute sessions in which groups of four students take turns explaining a worked problem to one another without a tutor present. Over one semester, 58 students attended circles and 61 attended standard tutor-led problem classes. Anxiety was measured with a 20-item scale at weeks 1 and 12, and attainment with the course's final examination. Anxiety fell by 11.6 points in the circles group and by 4.2 points in the comparison group. Final examination scores did not differ meaningfully (64.1% versus 63.3%). Interviews suggest that circles reduced fear of being judged by staff but that students were sometimes unsure whether a peer's explanation was correct.

1. Introduction

Many students enter introductory statistics with negative expectations formed in earlier mathematics education. Anxiety can lead to avoidance of help-seeking, late submission and disengagement from practice. Interventions have tended to focus on attainment, with anxiety measured as a secondary outcome if at all. This study reverses that emphasis and asks whether a low-cost social format reduces anxiety, and at what cost, if any, to attainment.

Research questions: RQ1: do peer-explanation circles reduce statistics anxiety more than tutor-led problem classes? RQ2: do they change final examination performance? RQ3: how do students experience explaining to peers rather than to staff?

2. Related Work

Explaining material to others, sometimes called the protege effect, has been linked to deeper processing (Okonkwo, 2017). Peer instruction in physics improved conceptual test scores in several cohorts (Brandt & Keller, 2019), though those studies included an instructor who resolved disagreements. For statistics specifically, Farrow (2021) reported that small-group work reduced anxiety but did not separate the effect of group size from the effect of tutor absence. Research on spaced retrieval and other individual revision strategies (Marlowe & Chen, 2019) has concentrated on memory outcomes and rarely reports affective measures. Haddad and Moreau (2022) caution that peer settings can spread misconceptions when no expert is present.

3. Method

Design and participants. Students on one introductory statistics module chose between two timetabled options at enrolment: peer-explanation circles (n = 58) or tutor-led problem classes (n = 61). Because students selected their own condition, the design is quasi-experimental.

Intervention. Each circle received a weekly problem sheet with a worked solution sealed in an envelope. Each student explained one part of the problem, the group compared answers, and only then opened the solution. A trained student facilitator kept time but did not explain content.

Measures. Anxiety was measured with the 20-item Statistics Worry Inventory (scores 20 to 100, higher meaning more anxious), a fictional instrument developed for this study. Attainment was the module's final examination. Twenty students (ten per condition) were interviewed in week 12.

Analysis. Change in anxiety was compared using ANCOVA with week-1 anxiety as a covariate. Interview transcripts were analysed thematically.

After adjusting for baseline anxiety, the circles group showed a larger reduction in anxiety (F(1,116) = 9.8, p = .002). Examination scores did not differ meaningfully. Attendance was higher in the circles group. Interviewees in circles described feeling able to ask "basic" questions, while six of ten said they sometimes left a session unsure whether the group's agreed answer had been right before the solution was opened.

5. Discussion

Circles appear to reduce anxiety without lowering attainment. The absence of an attainment gain contrasts with peer-instruction findings in physics (Brandt & Keller, 2019), possibly because those studies retained an instructor. The uncertainty students reported supports the caution raised by Haddad and Moreau (2022).

6. Limitations

Students chose their own condition, so more anxious students may have self-selected into circles, and the circles group began with higher anxiety. The anxiety scale is new and has not been validated. The study covers one module in one semester. We did not measure whether misconceptions formed in circles persisted, and we did not combine circles with any individual revision strategy.

7. Conclusion and Future Work

Peer-explanation circles were associated with a larger fall in statistics anxiety than tutor-led classes, with similar examination results. Future work should randomise assignment, validate the anxiety measure, add a brief expert check at the end of each circle, and test whether combining circles with individual retrieval practice improves both anxiety and attainment.
