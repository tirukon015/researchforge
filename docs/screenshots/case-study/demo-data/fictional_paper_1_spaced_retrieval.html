Spaced Retrieval Prompts for Undergraduate Statistics Revision: A Pilot Study

Amara Voss and Daniel Okafor
Department of Learning Sciences, Halden University (fictional)

Abstract

Students preparing for introductory statistics examinations commonly re-read notes, a strategy with weak evidence for durable learning. We report a six-week pilot in which 84 first-year students used a revision tool that delivered short retrieval questions at expanding intervals (1, 3, 7 and 14 days). A comparison group of 79 students re-read annotated lecture summaries for the same weekly time. On an unannounced delayed test four weeks after the final session, the retrieval group scored a mean of 71.4% against 62.9% for the re-reading group. Students in the retrieval group reported higher perceived effort and lower confidence during revision, despite scoring higher. Because assignment was by tutorial section rather than by individual, and the study ran in one course at one institution, the results are preliminary.

1. Introduction

Introductory statistics is a gateway course with high failure rates in many programmes. Revision strategies students choose on their own are often those that feel fluent, such as re-reading and highlighting, rather than those that produce durable memory. Retrieval practice, in which a learner recalls information instead of reviewing it, and spacing, in which practice is distributed over time, are two strategies with strong laboratory support. Less is known about whether a lightweight digital tool that combines them changes outcomes in an ordinary course, where attendance, motivation and workload vary.

This pilot asks three questions. RQ1: does spaced retrieval improve delayed test performance compared with re-reading for equal study time? RQ2: how do students perceive the effort and usefulness of each strategy? RQ3: which practical barriers affect sustained use of a retrieval tool over six weeks?

2. Related Work

Laboratory studies consistently report that retrieval practice outperforms restudy on delayed tests (Marlowe & Chen, 2019). Harrow and Lim (2021) extended this to a first-year biology course and found a benefit of similar size, but their comparison condition received less total study time, which may inflate the effect. Spacing effects are well established for vocabulary learning (Ibsen, 2018), yet evidence for conceptual and procedural material such as statistics is thinner. Quarterman et al. (2020) found that expanding intervals performed no better than fixed intervals for mathematics problems, contradicting earlier work on word lists.

A second line of work concerns student perception. Delacroix (2022) showed that students rate re-reading as more effective than retrieval even after scoring higher with retrieval, a pattern attributed to fluency being mistaken for learning. Finally, Novak and Achebe (2023) reported that engagement with voluntary revision apps falls sharply after the third week, which limits the practical reach of any tool-based intervention.

3. Method

Participants. 163 consenting students enrolled in an introductory statistics course took part. Eight tutorial sections were randomly assigned to conditions: four sections (n = 84) to spaced retrieval and four (n = 79) to re-reading.

Materials. The retrieval tool presented five short questions per session on topics already taught, covering sampling distributions, confidence intervals and hypothesis testing. Each question reappeared after 1, 3, 7 and 14 days unless answered incorrectly, in which case the interval reset. The re-reading group received annotated one-page summaries of the same topics.

Procedure. Both groups were asked to revise for 20 minutes three times per week for six weeks. Usage was logged by the tool and by a short weekly self-report. A 30-item delayed test was administered, unannounced, four weeks after the final session. Perceived effort and confidence were measured on 7-point scales at weeks 2 and 6, and twelve students from each group were interviewed at the end.

Analysis. Group means were compared with an independent-samples t-test on individual scores. Interviews were coded thematically by both authors.

The retrieval group scored 8.5 percentage points higher on the delayed test (t(161) = 4.19, p < .001). The advantage was largest for hypothesis-testing items and smallest for items on descriptive statistics. The retrieval group completed fewer sessions on average, with usage dropping most between weeks three and four. Students in the retrieval group described sessions as harder and reported lower confidence, yet 19 of the 24 interviewed students said they would use the tool again before the final examination.

Interviews identified three barriers to sustained use: notification fatigue, questions that felt repetitive once mastered, and uncertainty about whether a wrong answer meant a topic needed re-teaching.

5. Discussion

The pilot suggests that spaced retrieval can improve delayed performance in an ordinary statistics course even when students complete fewer sessions. The mismatch between confidence and performance mirrors Delacroix (2022) and suggests that students need to be told why retrieval feels harder. The decline in usage after week three is consistent with Novak and Achebe (2023).

6. Limitations

Assignment was by tutorial section, not by individual, so section-level differences such as tutor quality may confound the result, and the t-test did not account for clustering. The study ran in one course at one institution for six weeks. The delayed test was written by the course team and has not been validated. Usage data for the re-reading group came from self-report and may be inflated. We did not measure prior statistics knowledge.

7. Conclusion and Future Work

Spaced retrieval prompts outperformed re-reading on a delayed test in this pilot, although students found them harder and felt less confident. Future work should randomise at the individual level, adjust for clustering, test adaptive intervals that retire mastered questions, and examine whether brief explanations of desirable difficulty sustain engagement beyond the third week.
